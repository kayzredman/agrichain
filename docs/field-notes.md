# Field notes

Grouped from the conversation with Genevieve Leveille. These are the points she was making, not a transcript. The path they describe is drawn in the [blueprint](blueprint.md#path-of-a-lot).

## Fraud is a cartel business

This is not a farmer slipping five bad units into a crate. Containers move. Counterparties are unknown. Profits are funneled into other crime. Interpol, and units in India and Italy, already treat it as a case type. A monthly EU report lists seizures. The US hears less of it and mistakes that quiet for safety. Her worry beyond theft is use as a weapon: a poisoning or calorie siege is harder to see than a contaminated river, because the food simply arrives.

## The cases she uses

Seafood: about 20% fraudulent, including fish stamped as Greek that cannot have come from Greece, and toxic blowfish sold as another species. Horsemeat sold as beef, spotted early in Ireland, which pushed EU tagging rules. Chinese milk adulterated with melamine. Australia entered that market on the back of the scandal. Manuka and other New Zealand honey diluted with sucrose, dangerous for a diabetic who thinks they are eating honey. Powdered milk in Africa that is not what a mother thinks she is buying. Reported cases, she says, are far below what is happening.

## Origin is a processing trick

Rules of origin follow the place of most processing. The Netherlands can be a top food exporter of food it does not grow, because goods enter through Antwerp, get manufactured and bagged, and leave as European. Swiss chocolate has the same shape. Cashew is the sharp example: she cites Reuters that almost 90% of commercial cashews are grown in Côte d'Ivoire, and that the country does not process about 92% of them. Vietnam, India, and Cambodia crack and sort. She says the Ivorian price is driven by the Cambodian market, and that retail value is orders of magnitude above the farm. Store biological origin and processing origin as different facts.

## The blind middle, and the ship

Farm-to-fork rules, delayed and then arrived in Europe, track back to a first source. That still misses every hand between a thousand avocado growers and the supermarket, and it misses whether the factory shipped more than it received. A lot of food trade is still over the counter: brokers, phone days, ICC document sets, KYC questions whose answers do not fit. The chain can show factory to ship. It cannot, by itself, show whether the ship stopped, or whether the crew was trafficked. She ties underpaid maritime crew to the same cartels, and uses Qatar stadium labor as a reminder that trafficking is not only sexual exploitation.

## Fertilizer is the security story

She argues the coming squeeze is fertilizer, not crude. Ireland felt it early in the pandemic. Ukraine's fertilizer exports dropped out during the war. A blocked strait matters because refineries make the byproducts agriculture needs, and she says African buyers often lack those refineries. Yield models that ignore input shocks will misread both real shortages and fake abundance.

## Translucency, privacy, and who must join

A public window is the wrong goal. She credits Dave Birch for the banking lesson: glass windows let the public see the work and let an attacker see who sits where. Translucency means the record exists, and only someone with a right opens it. If a flow looks normal, nobody digs. EU farm-to-fork already struggles with identity and privacy, especially in coffee, tea, cocoa, and sugar. Adoption fails if the system is built for one company or one person who controls many farmers. It has to be open. Farmers and governments can understand it. The middle resists, because the middle is the opacity. Governments need a domestic reason: jobs and value retained at home, as in the CARICOM idea of processing in Guyana instead of shipping raw goods north and buying food back in dollars.

## Incentives, charity, and the next decade

She wants impact through capitalism. Charity makes the receiver serve the donor, and she says donor states pulled money home when their own people were under pressure. Ethiopia's recent growth and a self-funded dam are her contrast with IMF borrowing in a foreign currency. Tokenization without a reason for each participant is the trap she has already watched. She does not think AI for its own sake is enough: people remain part of the check. An average farmer age of 57 is, for her, an income problem. Younger people might farm if the day paid and still left a life.
